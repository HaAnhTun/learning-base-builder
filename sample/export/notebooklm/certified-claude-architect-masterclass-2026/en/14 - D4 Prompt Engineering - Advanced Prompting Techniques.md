A handful of terms anchor this lesson. A multiturn dialogue is a conversation across two or more exchanges where each turn builds on the prior response.

Together, these three concepts describe the core toolkit for designing effective multiturn interactions. Multiturn dialogue outperforms a single prompt in four predictable situations. First, when the task is ambiguous and any assumption the model makes could lead to wrong output that then needs rework.

**[01:18]** Second, when the desired final output depends on intermediate results the model produces along the way. Third, when a human needs to review and approve one step before the next begins, because that review step breaks the flow of a single call. Fourth, when the task is long enough that fitting everything into one call creates context, window pressure, and risks degraded quality near the end. When any one of these conditions exists, dialogue is the stronger architectural choice. The comparison between a single long prompt and a multiturn dialogue is worth making explicit. A single long prompt gives the model everything at once, which works well for well-defined tasks where the inputs are complete and clear.

**[02:02]** The problem is that single prompts fail badly when inputs are ambiguous, when outputs are interdependent across steps, or when the task benefits from a human review midway through. Multiturn dialogue addresses all three of those failure modes. Each turn can be smaller and more focused. You can course correct after each step. You can inject clarifications before a wrong assumption compounds. That flexibility is the real advantage of dialogue over a monolithic prompt.

**[02:31]** A good clarification turn is disciplined. It starts by acknowledging what the model understood from the user's input, so the user knows their message was processed correctly. Then it identifies the single ambiguity that is most likely to produce wrong output and asks exactly one targeted question about that ambiguity. It ends by setting an expectation for what comes next once the question is answered. Asking one question, rather than a list, is critical. A clarification turn with three or four questions feels like an intake form rather than a conversation, and users start to feel interrogated rather than helped.

**[03:03]** Pick the ambiguity that matters most and ask about only that one. The pitfalls in clarification turn design are predictable. Asking too many questions at once is the most common failure. Three or four open questions in a single turn create a form-filling experience, not a dialogue. Clarifying things the model could reasonably infer from context wastes a turn and signals poor judgment to the user. Skipping acknowledgment of what was understood and jumping straight to a question makes users feel ignored, and open-ended phishing questions like, Can you tell me more?

**[03:40]** generate rambling responses rather than the specific detail the model actually needs. Good clarification turns are short, specific, and respectful of the user's time. Each of these pitfalls shares the same root, failing to treat the clarification as a purposeful, targeted exchange rather than a data gathering exercise. Breaking a complex task into a turn sequence starts with identifying the output dependencies. Ask what each step needs from the prior step before it can proceed. **[04:12]** Assign one clear goal per turn rather than bundling multiple objectives into a single message. When you send a turn, use the model's prior output as explicit input.
