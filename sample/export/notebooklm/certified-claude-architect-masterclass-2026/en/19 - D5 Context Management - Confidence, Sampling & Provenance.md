That calibration is what turns a self-report into something you can route on.

**[03:34]** Choose which high-stakes fields get a confident sibling based on downstream risk. Once the model returns scores for each field your routing logic reads them against a threshold. ## 127. Calibrating with a Validation Set

> Course: Claude Certified Architect (CCAR-F) - 2026 Exam Prep · Lesson 127/136 · en_US · auto caption

**[00:00]** This lesson walks through calibration, verifying that a model's confidence scores actually match its real accuracy. We'll cover what calibration means, how to build a labeled validation set, how to run the calibration check, and how to read a reliability diagram to spot miscalibration. Let's get into it. A self-reported number is only worth what it predicts. Calibration is the check that decides whether a model's confidence scores predict anything at all. A validation set is a held-out collection of labeled samples you use to measure how accurate the extractions really are.

**[00:37]** A reliability diagram plots what the model claims against what it actually delivers. So miscalibration becomes something you can see, rather than something you suspect. And the routing threshold is the cutoff you set from that measured result, instead of from raw model output. Each one depends on the one before it, and together they turn a self-reported score into evidence you can route on. Calibration has a specific meaning worth stating precisely. A calibrated model is one where the score it reports matches its actual accuracy.

**[01:11]** If the model says 0.9 on a field type, a calibrated model is correct on that field type 90% of the time. That alignment is what you're checking for. An overconfident model says 0.9, but is only correct 60% of the time. It thinks it's doing well, but it's feeding errors into your auto-accept pipeline without any flag. An underconfident model says 0.6, but is actually correct 90% of the time. That wastes reviewer capacity on work that didn't need checking.

**[01:44]** Both miscalibrations are worth fixing, but overconfidence is the more dangerous failure, because it silently lets errors through. Building a useful validation set requires three things. First, the samples have to be representative of your actual production documents, not cherry-picked easy cases. If your production data includes handwritten forms, scanned invoices, and digital submissions, the validation set should include all of those. Second, every sample needs a ground truth label for each field you are measuring. A human expert creates those labels by checking the correct value against the source document.

**[02:23]** Third, the set stays held out. You never use it to train the model or adjust the prompt, because that would contaminate the measurement and make the calibration result unreliable. The contrast between an overconfident and a well-calibrated model shows up clearly in what happens downstream, and it's worth walking through both cases. When the model is overconfident, it reports high scores on fields, it frequently gets wrong. The system trusts those scores, auto-accepts the fields, and errors flow into production without anyone catching them. The system looks efficient on paper, but is silently degrading output quality.

**[03:04]** When the model is well-calibrated, a field scored at 0.85 is correct, roughly 85% of the time. The acceptance threshold you set at 0.9 actually corresponds to 90% accuracy in practice. That alignment is what makes per-field triage genuinely reliable, not just a mechanism for moving work around. Running the calibration check is a four-step process. Start by grouping the validation predictions into buckets, based on their confidence scores. A bucket for 0.0 to 0.1, another for 0.1 to 0.2, and so on up to 1.0.

**[03:44]** Then for each bucket, measure the fraction of predictions in that range that match the ground truth label. That fraction is the empirical accuracy for that confidence range. Next, plot each bucket's midpoint confidence on the x-axis against its empirical accuracy on the y-axis. Draw a diagonal line from the bottom left corner to the top right. The diagonal represents perfect calibration. Finally, find the gaps.

**[04:12]** Bins below the diagonal indicate overconfidence, and bins above show underconfidence. The distance from your plotted points to the diagonal tells you how miscalibrated the model is and where threshold adjustment is needed. The reliability diagram is the primary tool for communicating calibration results to anyone who sets routing policy. The x-axis runs from 0 to 1.0 and shows the model's reported confidence. The y-axis runs from 0 to 1.0 and shows the empirical accuracy measured on the validation set. A perfectly calibrated model traces the diagonal exactly, so confidence of 0.7 maps to accuracy of 0.7.

**[04:57]** When the plotted line curves above the diagonal, the model is underconfident in that range. When it curves below, the model is overconfident. A diagram that hugs the diagonal across all confidence ranges tells you the scores are trustworthy enough to drive routing decisions directly. Once you have the reliability diagram, adjusting the routing threshold is straightforward. Start with your policy's accuracy requirement. If the business needs at least 95% accuracy on auto-accepted fields, find the 0.95 level on the y-axis of the diagram.

**[05:32]** Trace horizontally to where your calibration curve crosses that accuracy level. Then read down to the x-axis to find the corresponding confidence value. Set that confidence value as your routing threshold. Fields scoring at or above it will achieve roughly 95% accuracy based on the calibration data. Fields below it go to review. The threshold now reflects real performance rather than the model's self-assessment.

**[06:00]** Calibration results don't stay valid indefinitely. When a team upgrades to a newer model version, the confidence distribution shifts because the new model generates different scores for the same inputs. When a prompt is revised, even a subtle change to phrasing or examples can move scores across the threshold boundary. Both situations require rerunning the calibration check on the same validation set. The validation set itself stays fixed, so the comparison is fair. Treating calibration as a recurring operational task, scheduled alongside model updates and prompt changes, keeps the routing thresholds honest over time rather than slowly drifting out of alignment.

**[06:40]** Calibration is what makes a confidence score actionable. A validation set supplies labeled ground truth. Binding predictions by score and measuring accuracy reveals where the model is over- or under-confident, and the reliability diagram makes those gaps visible enough to set a threshold against. Because a model upgrade or a prompt revision shifts the score distribution, this is recurring work rather than a one-time setup, so keep that in mind. With that, we'll wrap up this lesson. ## 128. Stratified Sampling — Why Random Misses What Matters

> Course: Claude Certified Architect (CCAR-F) - 2026 Exam Prep · Lesson 128/136 · en_US · auto caption

**[00:00]** This lesson covers stratified sampling for AI output review. We'll look at why random sampling misses what matters, how to stratify by confidence, by category, and by stakes, and how combining all three dimensions maximizes the real errors your reviewers catch per hour. Let's get into it. Random selection sounds fair, and that is exactly what makes it dangerous. A queue built at random fills up almost entirely with outputs. The model already got right.

**[00:30]** Stratified sampling is the alternative, a review selection strategy that deliberately oversamples the outputs most likely to be wrong. Weighting selection toward low confidence outputs, rare categories, and high stakes decisions. Stakes weighting says the importance of an error matters as much as the model's uncertainty. A confident wrong answer on a high stakes decision is every bit as dangerous as an uncertain one on a routine task. Reviewer burnout is what happens when neither of those is accounted for. Flood people with correct output and they stop reading carefully, which is a system design failure, not a people problem.

**[01:10]** Here's the core problem with random sampling. If your model is 95% accurate, then a random sample of 100 outputs will contain roughly 95 correct ones and only five errors. Your reviewers spend most of their time confirming what the model already knows, and the actual failure cases show up infrequently enough that patterns are hard to spot. Now scale that up. In a system processing 10,000 outputs per day, random review at a 1% rate gives you 100 reviews, of which maybe five contain real errors. You're burning reviewer time on low value confirmations.

**[01:47]** The errors you do catch are statistically isolated, so you can't identify the underlying cause. Random sampling gives you coverage on paper while hiding the actual failure distribution. Let's compare the two approaches directly. Random sampling is simple to implement. Every output has the same probability of selection, and no additional infrastructure is needed beyond a counter or a random number check. The downside is that the signal your reviewers receive is diluted by the model's existing accuracy.

**[02:19]** Stratified sampling requires more setup. You need confidence scores, category labels, and a sampling policy that specifies rates for each stratum. That's real engineering work, but the payoff is significant. Reviewers see a concentrated stream of uncertain, rare, or high-stakes outputs. The error density in the queue goes up. Reviewers catch more real problems per hour and can identify patterns across cases.

**[02:46]** The upfront investment in stratification pays off quickly at any reasonable review volume. The most common starting point for stratification is the confidence score. For a classifier, that score is the probability distribution it already outputs. For generative extraction, it's the verbalized self-report you elicit by asking, the same mechanism covered earlier, calibrated against a held-out set, so the numbers mean something. Whatever the source, you set a threshold, say 0.7, and any output below that threshold gets sampled at a higher rate. Outputs above the threshold still enter the review pool, but at a much lower rate.

**[03:27]** The threshold itself is tunable. If reviewers have excess capacity, lower it to pull in more borderline cases. If they're overwhelmed, raise it to focus only on the lowest confidence outputs. Confidence stratification alone will dramatically improve your reviewers' error yield compared to a purely random baseline. Stratification actually has three distinct dimensions, and effective systems use all three. The first is confidence.

**[03:54]** Anything below your threshold goes into a high-priority review lane. The second is category balance. If 80% of your outputs fall into one common category and only 5% land in a rare but important one, random sampling mirrors that imbalance, and the rare class stays underreviewed even when its error rate is far higher. Per-category quotas fix this. You set a minimum number of reviews per category per day, independent of volume, which also surfaces drift early when a previously clean category starts failing. The third dimension is stakes.

**[04:31]** Some outputs need review regardless of confidence because an error there is severe. These dimensions compose, so an output hitting two of them belongs at the top of the queue. Reviewer burnout deserves its own focus because it's a system design failure that looks like a personnel problem. When reviewers process hundreds of outputs per shift and the vast majority are correct, they develop a pattern of approving quickly. That's rational behavior given the signal they're receiving, but it means that when a real error does appear, the reviewer is less likely to catch it. Their cognitive threshold has been recalibrated by the volume of correct outputs.

**[05:11]** Stratification directly counters this by raising the error density of the review queue. When reviewers know that a meaningful fraction of every batch contains real issues, they stay engaged. The goal isn't to make the job harder. It's to make every review session worth the reviewer's full attention, rather than a rote confirmation exercise. Stratified sampling turns review from a coverage exercise into a targeted quality signal, concentrating reviewer attention on uncertain, rare, and high-stakes outputs, raises the number of real errors caught per hour, and gives you a much clearer picture of where the model actually struggles. The upfront work of scoring, labeling, and setting sample rates pays for itself quickly at any serious review volume.

**[05:59]** With that, we'll wrap up this lesson. I'll see you in the next one.

## 129. Routing Low-Confidence Output Through a Review Queue

> Course: Claude Certified Architect (CCAR-F) - 2026 Exam Prep · Lesson 129/136 · en_US · auto caption

**[00:00]** In this lesson we'll go through the reviewer queue as an operational system. We'll look at how low confidence outputs get routed in, what the reviewer interface should show, how approve, edit, and reject actions feed back into model improvement, and how to handle latency constraints and bypass scenarios. Let's get into it. A low confidence output has to go somewhere, and that somewhere is a review queue. It is a dedicated work surface, separate from your production pipeline, where flagged outputs land for human inspection. It isn't just a list of items.

**[00:34]** It is a system with priority ordering, context presentation, and action capture. The reviewer action set is what people do once they see an item, and it is deliberately narrow. Approve the output as correct, reject it entirely, or edit it into the correct answer. Ambiguous actions set slow reviewers down and produce inconsistent records. The bypass policy is the rule that lets certain outputs skip the queue, because not every situation can wait for a reviewer. Let's look at the queue architecture from the top.

**[01:07]** The queue sits between the model and whatever uses its output, whether that's an end user, a downstream system, or an automated process. When the model produces an output, a routing layer checks the confidence score against your stratification policy. If the output falls below threshold or hits a high stakes classification, it goes to the queue instead of being released. High confidence, low stakes outputs continue downstream without interruption. The queue holds flagged items in a prioritized list, with the lowest confidence and highest stakes outputs surfacing first. Items stay in the queue until a reviewer acts on them.

**[01:48]** The two operational metrics you track from day one are queue depth, how many items are waiting, and queue latency, how long items wait before review. The reviewer interface is where the system either succeeds or fails in practice. If reviewers have to hunt for context, they'll make faster decisions with less information, and the quality of those decisions will suffer. A well-designed interface puts everything on one screen. The original input that generated the model's output appears on the left or top. The model's output and its confidence score appear alongside it.

**[02:23]** If a secondary system or rule engine has generated a suggested correction, that shows up too. The reviewer reads the input, evaluates the output against it, considers the suggestion, and makes a decision, all without navigating away. That single screen constraint keeps reviews fast and keeps decision quality high. Anything that forces a tab switch or a search adds friction and degrades throughput. Each reviewer action carries a distinct meaning in the system's record. Approve means the reviewer has confirmed the output is correct and it can be released downstream.

**[02:58]** The approval is logged with a timestamp and reviewer identity. Edit means the output was wrong or incomplete and the reviewer has provided the correct version. The edited output gets released and the original plus correction pair goes into a data set for future training or calibration. Reject means the output is wrong and no corrected version should be released. Maybe the input itself was ambiguous or the correct answer requires information the reviewer doesn't have. Reject discards the item from the release path and flags the input type for further analysis.

**[03:33]** All three actions close the loop differently, but all three generate a logged record. Four design principles separate a functional review queue from one that reviewers game or ignore. The first is a single action surface. Reviewers should be able to approve, edit, or reject without opening another tool, searching a knowledge base, or switching context. Every additional step adds latency and introduces inconsistency. The second is context first.

**[03:58]** Input, output, and confidence score all need to be visible before the reviewer takes any action. Don't bury the confidence score in a metadata panel. Put it where reviewers see it immediately. The third is queue priority. High stakes and low confidence items should surface at the top of the reviewers list automatically. Reviewers should never have to sort their own queue.

**[04:25]** The fourth is the audit trail. Every action logs who reviewed the item, what changed, and when. The most underused feature of a review queue is the feedback loop it creates for model improvement. Every reviewer action is a labeled data point. When a reviewer approves an output, you've confirmed the model's behavior on that specific input type at that confidence level. That's a positive training signal.

**[04:49]** When a reviewer edits an output, you've captured the correct input-output pair. Edit corrections are high value training data because they come from real production inputs, not from curated data sets, and they're labeled by a domain expert who examined the specific failure. When a reviewer rejects an output, you've identified an input class the model handles poorly. Aggregated rejects point directly at the areas where the model needs additional training or where the input pre-processing needs rethinking. The queue pays for itself through the data it generates. Adding a review step creates latency, and you need to decide how to handle that before the system goes to production.

**[05:32]** Synchronous review means the downstream consumer waits for a reviewer decision before receiving the output. This is appropriate when the downstream process can tolerate variable response times, but it creates a hard dependency on reviewer availability. If no reviewer is active, the pipeline stalls. Asynchronous review returns a placeholder to the downstream consumer immediately and fulfills the actual output once a review decision is made. This keeps the pipeline moving, but requires the downstream system to handle deferred fulfillment. Some use cases work well with async, others don't.

**[06:08]** A medical documentation system that needs the correct output before a clinician signs off can't use a placeholder. Design the latency model explicitly and early before downstream integrations are built around assumptions. The bypass policy handles the cases where waiting for a reviewer isn't possible. There are three main scenarios where bypass is appropriate. Emergency override applies when a time-critical situation means that review delay causes more harm than the risk of an unreviewed output. This should be rare and tightly defined.

**[06:43]** A high confidence floor bypass allows outputs above a validated confidence floor to skip the queue automatically. The threshold has to be validated empirically, not set arbitrarily, and every bypass should still be logged. The third scenario is reviewer unavailability. If the queue has no active reviewers and the business needs continuity, a fallback rule can release outputs with a warning flag. In all three cases, the bypass action gets logged with the same completeness as a normal review decision. Bypass isn't an escape from accountability.

**[07:20]** A review queue is the infrastructure that makes human oversight scalable, auditable, and genuinely useful for model improvement. Routing, the reviewer interface, the action set, and the bypass policy all have to work together for the system to hold up under production load. Get any of these wrong and the queue becomes a delay rather than a quality control. That wraps up this lesson. I'll see you in the next one.

> Course: Claude Certified Architect (CCAR-F) - 2026 Exam Prep · Lesson 131/136 · en_US · auto caption

**[00:00]** In this lesson, we're going to talk about provenance tracking. When an AI model synthesizes information from multiple sources, the output reads confidently. Let's get into it. Read a synthesized report and you can't tell from the text alone which sentence came from which document.

**[00:31]** Provenance tracking is the practice that fixes that. It attaches a verifiable source pointer to every claim in the output. So fluency stops being the only thing you have to go on. Claim to source mapping is the data structure underneath it, linking each output claim to the specific source documents it came from. Inline citations are how that mapping surfaces in the readable text. An inline citation sits next to the claim it supports, which is a fundamentally different thing from a bibliography at the end of a paragraph.

**[01:03]** A bibliography tells you what sources exist. It doesn't tell you which sentence came from which one. Here's the core problem. A large language model is excellent at producing fluent, confident-sounding prose. When it synthesizes 10 source documents into a single report, the output reads like it was written by someone who read everything and understands it. But the sources themselves are invisible.

**[01:28]** As a reviewer, you have no direct way to know which sentence came from which document. That means you can't efficiently check whether a claim is accurate. Errors compound across a long document because each incorrect claim becomes a building block for the next. And when a regulator or an auditor asks you to show them where a conclusion came from, you are stuck. Provenance tracking solves this by making sources visible at the claim level. The choice between inline citations and end-of-paragraph lists isn't just cosmetic.

**[02:01]** It determines how granular your accountability is. Inline citations sit next to each claim. A reviewer can point to any sentence in the output, follow the source pointer, and verify that the cited document actually supports that sentence. That's per-claim accountability. End-of-paragraph citation lists tell you which sources informed a paragraph, but they don't tell you which specific sentence came from which source. That's paragraph-level accountability, at best.

**[02:28]** For high-stakes synthesis, like regulatory filings, clinical summaries, or legal analysis, you need the inline approach. End-of-paragraph lists are acceptable only when the cost of a wrong claim is low and review time is limited. Let's get concrete about what a claim-to-source mapping schema actually looks like. At minimum, each record holds three things. First, the claim text itself, the exact sentence or assertion from the output. Second, a list of source identifiers, the document IDs, URLs, or chunk references for every source that supports that claim.

**[03:06]** Those identifiers have to be stable, meaning they don't break when a document is updated or moved. Unstable identifiers make the whole provenance trail unreliable. Third, an optional confidence score per source. You don't need to display any of this in the readable output. The schema lives in the background, but because each claim is now a first-class data object, you can query it, audit it, and update it independently of the surrounding text. There are four concrete reasons organizations should invest in provenance tracking.

**[03:40]** The first is trust. When stakeholders can verify a sample of claims, they are more willing to act on the synthesis. They don't need to verify everything, just enough to build confidence. The second is auditability. A regulator can request a trace of any fact to its origin without rerunning the model. The third is compliance, GDPR transparency and access obligations.

**[04:04]** In Articles 13 through 15, and the EU AI Act increasingly push organizations to explain the logic behind automated conclusions, not just what they concluded. The fourth is error correction. When a claim is flagged as wrong, you follow its source pointer directly to the problem. You don't need to resynthesize the whole document to fix one sentence. A verification workflow puts the provenance schema to work, and the structured version of it is called a sampling audit. The core idea is sampling.

**[04:38]** You don't verify every claim. Instead, you select a representative sample weighted toward higher risk claims, and you confirm each one against its source pointer. The process looks like this. Pick a claim from the output, follow the source identifiers to the cited documents, read the relevant passage, and judge whether the synthesized claim accurately reflects what the source says. There's no magic percentage. The right sample size depends on how common errors are, how much error you can tolerate, and the confidence level you need.

**[05:11]** A risk-weighted sample can catch systematic problems, including fabricated citations and distorted paraphrases, without requiring a human to read every source document in full. Fabricated citations deserve extra attention because they're particularly hard to detect without a verification workflow. Here's what happens. The model produces a fluent claim and attaches a real-looking source reference. The document title looks plausible, the authors sound credible, the date is recent. But when a reviewer follows the pointer, one of two things is true.

**[05:48]** Either the document doesn't support the claim that was made or the document doesn't exist at all. Reviewers who trust the citation format without verifying it will accept the claim as sourced. This is a systematic risk in any synthesis pipeline that doesn't enforce source verification. Provenance tracking turns synthesis from a black box into an auditable process.

**[06:22]** When every claim carries a source pointer, a reviewer can verify any sentence, an auditor can trace any fact back to its origin, and a wrong claim can be corrected one sentence at a time instead of by resynthesizing the entire document. ## 132. Conflict Annotation — Surfacing Disagreement in Synthesis

> Course: Claude Certified Architect (CCAR-F) - 2026 Exam Prep · Lesson 132/136 · en_US · auto caption

**[00:00]** In this lesson, we're looking at what happens when your source documents disagree. A well-designed synthesis pipeline doesn't just pick one source and move on. It detects the conflict, annotates it, and applies an explicit resolution strategy. We'll cover detection, annotation patterns, resolution rules, and logging disagreements even after they're resolved. Let's get into it. Your sources disagree.

**[00:23]** What does the output say? Conflict annotation is the practice of surfacing that disagreement rather than hiding it. When sources contradict each other, an annotated synthesis tells the reader a conflict exists instead of pretending only one source ever spoke. A resolution strategy is the explicit rule you use to decide between them. Prefer newer, prefer authoritative, and escalate to human are the main options, and we'll walk through each one. The averaging anti-pattern is the failure mode all of this guards against.

**[00:57]** It's what happens when a system silently blends conflicting sources or picks one without logging the choice. The reader gets a confident-sounding claim with no indication that another source said something different. The averaging anti-pattern is subtle because the output still looks good. The prose is fluent, the claim sounds certain, and there's nothing in the text to suggest that another source disagreed. Here's what actually happened. The model encountered two incompatible claims.

**[01:28]** Instead of flagging the conflict, it either averaged them into a vague middle claim that neither source actually supports, or it chose one, discarded the other, and presented that choice as settled fact. The reader has no way to know a choice was made. They can't evaluate whether the right source was selected, and the discarded source may have been more accurate, more recent, or more authoritative. Downstream decisions get made on a foundation of false certainty. This is why conflict annotation isn't optional in high-stakes synthesis. The cost of hidden disagreement is higher than the cost of surfacing it.

**[02:06]** Conflict detection is the upstream step that makes annotation possible. Before your pipeline synthesizes anything, it needs a mechanism to identify when two sources make incompatible claims about the same fact. The practical approach combines two techniques. First, semantic similarity. You find passages from different sources that are discussing the same topic. High similarity means they're making claims about the same thing.

**[02:32]** Second, entailment checking. You test whether one passage contradicts the other, supports it, or is simply unrelated. When two passages are semantically similar, but entailment scoring says they contradict each other, you have a detected conflict. The key insight is that detection should happen before synthesis, not after. If you wait until the output is already written, you're auditing a problem that's already been hidden. The comparison between detected and undetected conflicts comes down to what the reader is allowed to know.

**[03:05]** When a conflict is detected and annotated, the output tells the reader something like this. Source A reports that the policy was updated in 2022. Source B shows the policy was last revised in 2019. That difference hasn't been resolved. The reader now knows uncertainty exists. They can investigate, escalate, or accept the ambiguity with full knowledge.

**[03:27]** When a conflict goes undetected or unannounced, the reader sees a confident assertion with no indication that another source said something different. One of those two reading experiences produces informed decisions. The other produces confident mistakes. Conflict annotation is the difference between an honest synthesis and a misleading one. Once a conflict is detected, your pipeline needs a rule for resolving it. There are three main strategies.

**[03:54]** PreferNewer says that when two sources conflict, the more recent one wins. This works well for domains where facts evolve over time. Regulatory requirements, software versions, and pricing data all fit this pattern. PreferAuthoritative says that designated high-trust sources outrank secondary ones. You need to define an authority hierarchy for your source corpus in advance. Authoritative sources might be official standards, bodies, regulatory agencies, or primary research.

**[04:27]** EscalateToHuman is the third option. When neither PreferNewer nor PreferAuthoritative can cleanly resolve a conflict, you route the decision to a human reviewer. This is expensive but appropriate when the stakes are high enough that an automated rule could get it wrong. Here's a rule that's easy to overlook. When you resolve a conflict, you should log it, even though the conflict is now closed. The resolution trail needs to be preserved for future reference.

**[04:57]** Your conflict log should record the conflicting claims and their source identifiers, the resolution strategy that was applied, and which claim was selected alongside the one that was discarded. Why does this matter after the fact? Because future reviewers need to know that a choice was made. If the conflict log doesn't exist, a reviewer reading the final output three months later will see a confident claim and assume there was consensus. With the log, they see that two sources disagreed, a rule was applied, and a specific choice was recorded. That's the difference between an auditable system and an opaque one.

**[05:38]** Conflict annotation is only useful if readers actually engage with it. That means the presentation has to be calibrated to the stakes and the audience. For high stakes claims, like legal conclusions or clinical findings, surface the conflict inline. Put it right next to the claim it affects. For medium risk conflicts, use a footnote or a collapsible detail block. The main body stays readable, but the detail is available one click away.

**[06:06]** For full document review, generate a conflict summary at the end. This gives senior reviewers a consolidated list of all detected disagreements without forcing them to read the entire document. The goal across all of these approaches is the same. Readers who need to know about a conflict should be able to find it without digging, and readers who don't need it shouldn't be buried in noise.

**[06:31]** Synthesis that hides disagreements makes a choice the reader can't see. Detection catches the conflict before the output is written. Annotation surfaces it. An explicit resolution strategy decides between the competing claims. And the conflict log preserves the trail, so a later reviewer knows a choice was made rather than assuming consensus. Surfaced conflict beats invisible certainty every single time.
